# Starch coatings, batter and sauce thickening

First identify whether starch coats raw meat or thickens liquid in the pan; a thin coating, a thicker batter and a sauce slurry serve different purposes.

## Scope

Comparing the thin coating on scallion pork chops, starch-marinated meat in rice-cooker meals and the slurry used to finish braised aubergine.

## Steps

1. Identify the goal: a thin coating on marinated meat treats its surface, a thicker batter forms an outer shell on fried food, and a starch slurry added to hot liquid thickens the sauce.
2. For scallion pork chops, first mix in the prescribed seasonings and water and marinate, then add sweet potato starch for a thin coating rather than a separate thick batter.
3. For the beef or chicken in rice-cooker meals, add starch and oil as directed by that preparation, then brown and finish cooking with the rice. Do not transfer quantities between preparations.
4. For braised aubergine, prepare a separate starch slurry and stir it in after combining the ingredients with the sauce, watching for an even coating.

## Reasons

- Starch’s behaviour with water and heat can provide a coating or thicken a sauce, but placement, thickness and purpose differ. A thin coating contributes to the intended texture rather than forming a waterproof seal around all meat juices.
- Coating mixtures vary and do not always contain egg white. Baking soda changes the chemical environment and is distinct from starch coating; do not add it automatically whenever a recipe includes starch.

## Common mistakes

- Replacing a meat coating with sauce slurry, or covering thinly coated meat in thick batter, changes texture and cooking behaviour.
- Assuming a recipe with starch is missing egg white or baking soda and adding them without direction.

## Limits

This compares starch uses in existing recipes, not a universal coating ratio or a deep-frying recipe. A set or browned surface does not replace an internal doneness check.
