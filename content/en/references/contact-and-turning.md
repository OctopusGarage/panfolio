# Searing fish and meat: contact and turning

Gently press fish or chicken skin against the pan at first, release once set, then wait for browning and release before following the dish’s turning sequence.

## Scope

Searing skin-on salmon, whole skin-on chicken thighs and steak.

## Steps

1. Gently press lifted areas at the start to keep the skin against the pan.
2. Release pressure once the skin sets and leave it undisturbed.
3. Turn when the surface sets and releases naturally; do not press chicken after turning.
4. For steak, brown the surface first, then lower the heat and turn every 30–60 seconds to control center doneness.

## Reasons

- Moving the fish before its surface sets can make it stick and tear. Wait for natural release before turning. Skinless fish should also be left until its surface sets.
- Gentle pressure keeps the skin against the pan; it should not flatten the meat. Release the pressure once the skin sets and wait for natural release before turning to avoid tearing.
- Chicken needs medium-low heat to release fat beneath the skin; lower the heat after turning and stop pressing. Steak instead uses regular turning over low heat after both sides brown. Do not apply the thigh’s limited turning to this later steak stage.
- Turning off the heat, removing food from the pan and resting are different actions. The hot pan continues heating the contact surface, and the interior of thick meat may keep changing temperature after removal. Complete the recipe’s centre-temperature check before removing it rather than guessing that carryover heat will finish the cooking.

## Common mistakes

- Moving food immediately can make it stick and tear the skin.
- Heavy pressure or pressing after turning chicken can squeeze out juices.
- Forcing food off the pan immediately can tear a surface that has not set. Check browning and release rather than pressing harder instead of waiting.

## Limits

Pressure establishes contact rather than squeezing the food; natural release cues turning, not proof of center doneness. Regular turning after steak browns is a different stage from initial undisturbed browning.
